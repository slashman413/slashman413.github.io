AI is moving from answering questions to taking actions, and the announcements clustered on October 20 show the supporting paperwork arriving faster than the models improve. The interesting question is no longer whether an agent can call a restaurant, a data source, or a robot arm, but who authorized the call, what it was allowed to see, and how anyone proves afterward what happened.

## Delegation becomes the interface

Yelp’s new AI features include an agent that can call a restaurant to check wait times. That sounds minor until you notice what changed: the app is no longer just displaying information; it is acting under a user’s identity in the physical world. Figma co-founder Dylan Field’s comment that MCP is “not a perfect protocol” points to the same gap on the enterprise side. Figma says it will support as many standards as possible without exposing all of its data. In other words, the protocol is only the handshake. The real product is the permission model around it.

This matters because delegated action turns software errors into relationship errors. A wrong recommendation is annoying; a wrongly placed call, booking, or data request can cost money or trust. Agents therefore need scoped credentials, expiration, logs, and a clear way to revoke access. The companies that make those controls legible will capture value even if the underlying model is interchangeable.

## Boundaries are the product

The US order for federal civilian agencies to remove DeepSeek products and third-party services built on its technology shows that AI supply chains are now treated as security boundaries. The cited concern is national security, but the operational lesson is broader: organizations will adopt agentic tools only when they can trace which models, vendors, and subprocessors touch which data. Figma’s cautious stance on MCP reflects the same instinct at commercial scale. A protocol may be global; permissions are local.

Physical systems raise the stakes. Eka Robotics, reportedly valued at $1.45 billion, has impressed experts with a robotic arm that smoothly picks up a wide range of objects. Reliable Robotics, founded by SpaceX alumni, extended its Series D with $115 million in equity and $25 million in debt, topping a $1 billion valuation. A smooth demo is not the same as a deployable system. In aviation and manipulation, delegation requires certified safety envelopes: what the system may do, when it must stop, and who is accountable when it fails.

## The economics of acting on someone’s behalf

When an intermediary acts, revenue splits and data access become contract terms. Spotify’s paid podcast memberships let creators offer ad-free listening, early access, and bonus content while keeping 90% of revenue. That is a direct billing relationship, but it also creates new questions about how AI-mediated discovery or summaries share value. Oura’s new workout metrics show the same pattern in health data: the ring’s value depends on translating activity into guidance users trust, not just collecting steps.

Capital is following the operational proof. Reliable Robotics and Eka are being funded for systems that must work in constrained environments, not for general-purpose chat. Inspired Capital’s $450 million fourth fund, meanwhile, suggests investors see room for venture hubs beyond Silicon Valley, but the test is similar: can a company show repeatable deployment, clear liability, and defensible distribution? In agentic products, distribution and trust are intertwined.

## The audit layer to watch

The next competitive layer is likely to be the delegation ledger: a record of which agent acted, under whose identity, with what data, at what price, and with what outcome. Consumers will want a simple dashboard of permissions and an undo button. Enterprises will want exportable logs, third-party certification, and contracts that assign liability. Regulators will want to know which model touched protected data and whether the agency or vendor can prove it stopped.

That is why the MCP debate, the DeepSeek removal, Yelp’s calling agent, and the robotics valuations belong in the same frame. They are all tests of whether AI can be trusted to act, not just answer. The winners will be the systems that make delegated action boring: visible, bounded, reversible, and insured. The model may be impressive; the audit trail is what makes it usable.
