A quiet pattern runs through this week's technology news: when institutions confront uncertainty about AI, they rarely change the system. They add an exception. A private mode, a liability disclaimer, a conditional guarantee, a promise of editorial independence. Each is a carve-out — and each shifts the unresolved question somewhere else.

## Privacy becomes a setting, not a property

Google's AI Mode in Search now has a temporary-thread option: conversations can run without being saved to search history or used to shape future recommendations. That is a genuine improvement for anyone researching something sensitive — a medical question, a legal worry, a job search they would rather not advertise to an advertising system.

But notice the structure. The logged, personalized conversation remains the default; anonymity is a mode the user must know about and actively select. Carve-outs of this kind are useful precisely because they are cheap to build and easy to describe. They are also easy to leave unused. The people most likely to need a private research channel are often the least likely to know the toggle exists, which is why the difference between "we offer privacy" and "privacy is the default" matters more than any single feature announcement.

## Liability is being relocated to the person who delegates

Banks are moving in the same direction, with higher stakes. Citi is the latest major institution to signal that customers who hand financial tasks to third-party AI agents do so at their own risk — including, reportedly, when those agents are compromised. The reasoning is not unreasonable from the bank's side: it cannot audit software it did not build, and an agent holding credentials looks, to its systems, exactly like the customer.

What that produces is a carve-out in the other direction. The bank defines the boundary of authorized activity; everything outside it becomes the customer's problem. This is worth understanding before delegating anything with money attached: use accounts segregated from primary savings, keep human approval on transfers above a threshold, and read the terms that describe what counts as authorized. The practical effect of agentic finance may be less about convenience than about where losses land when something goes wrong.

## Capital with conditions attached

Nvidia offered a cleaner example of the same logic. After questions about how far it would finance the "neocloud" data-center operators that buy its hardware, interim CEO Colette Kress described a backstop that applies only if the operator has independent financing and actually gets the facility built with Nvidia equipment installed. Read plainly: the guarantee covers the scenario where things work, and not the scenario where a half-finished building sits empty.

That is sensible risk management for Nvidia. It is also a reminder that headline numbers about AI infrastructure investment often describe conditional commitments rather than committed cash. When a supplier also acts as financier, the difference between "backed" and "backed if" is the entire question.

## Promises are not mechanisms

The remaining examples are softer but related. David Ellison said HBO will still air a documentary critical of Elon Musk despite a $1 billion investment in Skydance — an assurance that editorial independence survives a financial relationship that would make many companies flinch. Meanwhile, Sam Altman and Greg Brockman pushed back on Silicon Valley's framing of an AI "takeover" while conceding how hard it is to keep advanced models under human control.

Both are commitments to preserve human judgment in systems where incentives point elsewhere. They may well be sincere. But a promise differs from a mechanism: it depends on disclosure, on someone being willing to absorb a cost, and on the people making it remaining in place.

## Why this matters

Carve-outs are not failures. They let organizations ship useful capabilities before every structural question is settled, and that is often better than waiting. The risk is that exceptions accumulate into a system where the default is permissive and the safe path is opt-in, where liability migrates to whoever has the least information, and where the difference between a guarantee and a gesture is only visible in fine print.

The useful questions are consistent across all four cases: what happens if you do nothing, who absorbs the loss, what triggers the exception, and can an outsider verify it? Institutions are answering the first question with "the more convenient option." The other three are still largely open.
