The week's headlines don't share a topic, but they share a question. A board is dissolved, a $7.5 billion stock sale is cancelled, a productivity company buys a notetaker, and a venture firm explains why it refuses to pick a favorite AI lab. Each item is a decision about how concentrated a single bet should be — and who absorbs the loss if it goes wrong.

## When your product is someone else's roadmap

TechCrunch Disrupt 2026 is promoting a session built around a question every AI founder now faces: what happens when OpenAI ships your roadmap? The framing is blunt — the greatest risk isn't building a weak product, it is building a strong one that eventually becomes someone else's feature. That is a dependency problem stated precisely. If your differentiation sits in a layer a foundation-model vendor can absorb in a single release cycle, your business is a concentrated bet on that vendor's indifference or slowness, and neither is something you can contract for.

Superhuman's acquisition of Fathom — a YC-backed notetaker with more than 400,000 monthly active users — is one response: pay for distribution and workflow context, the parts of the stack that model providers have less reason to own outright. Whether that insulation holds is an open question. The logic of buying your way into a wider position is not.

## Governance concentration and its limits

At Automattic, sources told TechCrunch the company's board is out, days after board members voted to put CEO Matt Mullenweg on leave. The reporting is sourced rather than company-confirmed, and the sequence of events is still thin. But the structural point is familiar. In founder-controlled companies, supervoting shares often concentrate final authority in one person, and a board that tries to act as a check can find itself replaced rather than obeyed. That is not automatically bad governance; founder control has genuine long-horizon uses in software. It is, however, a concentration risk that employees, customers, and partners hold whether or not they chose it — and one they cannot diversify away.

## The diversified position and its price

Insight Partners' Deven Parekh told TechCrunch his $90 billion firm is deliberately staying diversified — even holding stakes in rival AI labs — while much of the industry piles into OpenAI and Anthropic. Diversification has a cost: you surrender the outsized return that comes from being concentrated and right. It also has a benefit that is easier to appreciate when model leadership changes hands, since no single vendor's bad quarter becomes yours. 

Larry Ellison, meanwhile, cancelled a previously disclosed sale of 50 million Oracle shares worth roughly $7.5 billion. Insider-sale cancellations rarely come with explanations, and the possible motives — tax timing, signaling, a changed plan — are not discernible from outside. What is observable is the direction: a holder of an extremely concentrated stake chose not to shrink it. Cancelling a sale is not the same as buying more, and it does little to change how concentrated that position remains.

## Bets that can't be spread

Not everything can be hedged. AI-assisted de-extinction, the subject of another Disrupt session, is a billion-dollar, decades-long wager on outcomes that arrive — if at all — after many of the people who funded it have moved on. Advances in genetics and computational biology make it newly plausible; they do not make it diversifiable, and they do not answer the question the session itself raises: if technology can revive an extinct species, should it?

At the other end of the scale, a startup run out of a vinyl bar in Shibuya is shipping experimental music apps with no AI prompting at all, from a former Spotify executive. Refusing the frontier-model dependency is itself a strategy — a narrow, deliberate bet on a particular kind of user experience. It may not scale like a platform. It also cannot be absorbed by one.

The common thread is that the size of a bet is becoming a disclosed feature of a business rather than a background assumption. Founders, boards, and investors are being asked the same question in different forms: what happens to you if the one thing you depend on changes? Companies that can answer concretely — because they own the layer, because their governance is legible, because their positions are spread — will find it easier to raise, hire, and sell. Those that cannot will keep discovering the answer in public.
