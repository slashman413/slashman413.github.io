## The Threat: From Phishing to Phone Calls

Google's security researchers have uncovered a worrying trend: hackers are now calling financial firm employees directly to hack and extort them. This is a departure from typical phishing emails or malware drops—it's a personal, voice-driven attack that exploits human trust. By combining social engineering with technical exploits, these groups are breaking into large U.S. financial institutions, stealing sensitive data, and demanding payment. The use of phone calls adds a new layer of sophistication that many security training programs fail to address. Meanwhile, the China-linked LightSpy spyware has been caught targeting victims in 13 countries, including the United States. According to researchers, one operator even placed a KFC order using their real name and office address, highlighting the often-ramshackle nature of these operations—yet the impact is anything but sloppy. These incidents underscore that cybersecurity is no longer just about patching software; it's about defending against a spectrum of physical and social vectors. For enterprises, this means investing in real-time monitoring, employee awareness, and incident response that can handle both digital and voice-based attacks.

Hadrian's approach—using automation and advanced robotics to produce precision components—could reduce reliance on a fragile supply chain that has been exposed by geopolitical tensions. It's a sharp contrast to the typical software-first startup, but it reflects a growing recognition that tech's next battlefield is physical production. Similarly, Ford's new Fathom EV pickup, priced at $30,000, is aimed at the mass market. Both Hadrian and Ford highlight a fundamental truth: innovation in the physical world is slow, capital-intensive, and unforgiving—but it's essential.
