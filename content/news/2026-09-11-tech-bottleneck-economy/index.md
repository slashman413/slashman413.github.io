On September 10, a batch of announcements landed that appear to have nothing in common: a venture firm taking a stake in a professional soccer club, a chipmaker projecting 70% growth, a chatbot company pausing new paid signups. Read together, they describe an industry reorganizing around one question — who controls the input that everyone else needs?

## Compute and materials: scarcity moves upstream

Nvidia's Jensen Huang told TechCrunch he expects another year of extraordinary growth and preemptively rejected the charge that the company's deals are circular. The defense matters more than the projection. If the AI buildout is partly financed by the same vendors selling into it, then reported demand reflects financial engineering as much as end-user appetite. Huang's insistence that it does not is a claim about where the money ultimately originates — and it is the single most load-bearing assumption in the sector right now.

A quieter story points at an older kind of bottleneck. Proxima Fusion, a German startup, plans to spend €140 million on a factory for fusion-grade high-temperature superconducting tape, an input currently dominated by Asian suppliers. Fusion timelines are usually discussed as physics problems. Proxima's bet suggests they are manufacturing problems: you cannot build reactors faster than you can make the material inside them.

## When demand outruns capacity, growth becomes rationing

OpenAI has paused new Pro subscriptions, citing the strain that demand for its Astra product puts on its systems. That is an unusual posture for consumer software, where scaling has generally been a matter of spending more on servers. It is normal, however, for utilities and airlines: when capacity is the binding constraint, you manage the queue rather than the funnel.

Compare Meta, whose new AI agent app Muse reportedly reached No. 2 in the U.S. app rankings despite a slower start than the company's earlier launches. Meta does not have to ration attention; it owns distribution channels that most competitors must rent. The asymmetry is instructive — OpenAI is constrained by compute, Meta by engagement. In both cases, the scarce resource, not the model, sets the strategy.

## Distillation and the limits of imitation

Anthropic published a report alleging persistent "distillation" campaigns by Alibaba, Moonshot AI, and DeepSeek — efforts to extract a model's capabilities through its API and use them to train rivals. Whatever one concludes about specific accusations, the structural problem is clear: a model that answers questions is also a machine for exporting its own behavior, and terms of service are thin protection across jurisdictions.

The practical consequence is that a frontier lab's durable advantage is less the current weights than the cadence of improvement. If imitation lags by a generation, distillation is survivable; if it closes the gap to months, the business model gets much harder.

## Capital still follows narrative

Collaborative Fund's purchase of a stake in D.C. United and its stadium is being pitched as a showcase for portfolio startups — a venue, a network, and a story in one asset. Thrive Capital's earlier move into sports ownership suggested the same logic. These are not primarily sports investments; they are marketing and relationship infrastructure bought with fund capital.

Meanwhile, the founders of German energy startup Furo left Silicon Valley and still raised $4 million, mostly from U.S. backers. Talent and capital can now find each other across an ocean, but the network that makes the introduction still concentrates in a handful of places. Mark Wahlberg's appearance at Disrupt 2026 to discuss investing and entrepreneurship fits the same pattern: attention is a distribution channel, and celebrities own a lot of it.

## Why it matters

The through-line is unglamorous. Compute, superconducting tape, inference capacity, distribution, and access to the right rooms are all finite, and the firms positioned near those bottlenecks capture disproportionate value. Product ideas are cheap. What determines outcomes in this cycle is who can guarantee supply, ration demand, and slow imitation — and who has to queue.
