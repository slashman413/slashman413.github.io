On a single day, eight technology stories described eight unrelated businesses: website hosting, web browsing, social media, semiconductors, ebooks, satellite broadband, AI research and chatbots. Read together, they sketch one market. The money is moving away from capturing audiences and toward controlling inputs — the physical components, spectrum, licensed text, proprietary data and compute that everything else is built from.

## Buying the bottleneck instead of renting it

The largest signal is Marvell's reported $115 billion purchase of Lumentum, one of the biggest acquisitions in tech history. Lumentum makes optical components that sit between AI accelerators and the networks connecting them; when demand for datacenter interconnect outruns supply, owning the supplier is worth more than bidding for its output. Notably, Nvidia is a strategic investor in both companies, which means the deal tightens a link in a supply chain Nvidia already depends on.

SpaceX's reported interest in Boost Mobile follows the same logic in a different medium. Starlink has orbital capacity and a growing subscriber base, but competing with AT&T, T-Mobile and Verizon requires terrestrial spectrum and retail distribution — inputs that take years to acquire through regulators and far less time to buy. The talks are reported, not confirmed, and any deal would face spectrum and antitrust review.

The smaller examples rhyme. Readwise's new Books app bundles an ebook store, a reading tracker, detailed statistics and AI features; the differentiator is not the feature list but the store itself, which reduces dependence on a competitor's catalog and APIs. And 42AI emerged from stealth with $62.5 million for a "world model" that claims to predict human behavior in unfamiliar situations — a pitch that only works if the underlying data and simulation are proprietary rather than rented.

## When inputs are priced by courts and contracts

The New York Times and other outlets suing Brave over alleged scraping make the same point from the opposite direction. The claim is that the browser collected copyrighted material and passed it to AI customers such as Anthropic and Perplexity. Whatever the merits, the practical function of such suits is to convert freely crawlable text into a licensed input with a negotiable price. Litigation is becoming a pricing mechanism for raw material that markets never priced.

Snap's settlement of a suit alleging its platform fueled teen depression and eating disorders belongs on the same ledger, though it concerns design rather than data. Plaintiffs pointed to autoplay and infinite scrolling as drivers of compulsive use. Settlement terms were not disclosed, but the precedent matters: engagement features now carry a provisioning cost — legal, product and reputational — that has to be booked alongside server bills.

## The cost side becomes visible

The clearest admission that inputs are no longer cheap came from Elon Musk, who throttled usage limits on Grok Bot because costs were "unsustainably high" despite what he called the fastest growth of any product he has launched. Unlimited inference is a subsidy, and subsidies end when the meter runs faster than revenue.

Squarespace's 19% layoff, framed as organizational restructuring, points at the other side of the same equation. A business that rents most of its inputs — cloud capacity, ad inventory, engineering talent — and resells convenience has limited room to absorb higher costs, so it cuts headcount to protect margin. Companies without a proprietary input tend to compete on efficiency; companies that own one can compete on price.

## Why the shift matters

For consumers, the likely near-term effect is more tiered pricing: metered AI usage, higher connectivity bundles, subscriptions that bundle content because the content itself now carries a license fee. Free-at-the-point-of-use was a function of underpriced inputs.

For competition, vertical integration raises the height of the wall around each market. A startup can still win, but increasingly it must either own a narrow input — a dataset, a component, a niche catalog — or pay rent to someone who does. That is a different playbook from the growth-era default of acquiring users first and worrying about supply later.

For regulators, the questions are concrete: will the Marvell–Lumentum deal pass competition review, will SpaceX be allowed to convert spectrum into a national wireless business, and will courts effectively set the price of training data? Watch those three, and the shape of the next few years becomes legible. The audience is still valuable, but it is no longer the scarce thing.
