This week’s headlines reveal a subtle pattern: as artificial intelligence expands its reach, the key leverage points are shifting from raw computational power to who owns the data, sets the infrastructure rules, and designs the physical interfaces that mediate human-AI interaction.

## The Battle Over Data and Open Science

The lawsuit filed by Sony Music and Warner against Anthropic is the latest in a series of legal clashes over copyrighted material in AI training sets. The labels allege a “brazen campaign” of intellectual property theft, framing the issue as a question of ownership and consent. This is a clear struggle for control over the creative data that fuels generative models. Meanwhile, Vijay Pande, the former a16z biotech lead who now runs the small AI-native VC fund VZVC, argues that medicine will not be transformed by walled-off datasets but by open, shared ones. His position—that biology is shifting from discovery to engineering—implies that data control must be distributed to unlock full potential. These two perspectives bracket the central tension of the AI economy: whether control should be concentrated in the hands of rights holders or broadened to accelerate innovation.

## Infrastructure and Physical Manifestations

Control is also being contested at the infrastructure and hardware layers. Nvidia’s AI advantage is no longer just about the GPU; the company is now focusing on smarter traffic control across data center systems, suggesting that efficiency gains will come from managing the flow of data, not just adding more processors. Chinese automakers are following Tesla’s bet that humanoid robots are the next major profit engine—a move that extends AI control from digital logic to physical labor. At the consumer level, XREAL’s smart glasses offer an alternative way to watch movies, embedding AI and display technology into a pair of sunglasses. Each of these developments represents an attempt to establish chokepoints—whether in network orchestration, robotic actuators, or the user’s visual field—that will shape how AI is delivered and experienced.

## Human Agency and the Everyday Body

At TechBBQ, Europe’s annual tech conference, the recurring question was: who is actually in control? Investors, founders, and operators pressed for human agency over AI, reflecting a growing anxiety about the systems that increasingly mediate work and life. That concern is mirrored in more mundane products. The Theragun Sense, a consumer massage gun, markets itself around making recovery from aches and pains easier—a form of regaining control over one’s own body in an era of sedentary, screen-centric lifestyles. Meanwhile, Hollywood celebrities entering the microdrama app space signals a shift in content control; rather than relying on massive studios and eight-figure checks, they are embracing short, direct-to-audience formats that bypass traditional gatekeepers. These examples, however disparate, all point to a desire for individual and collective agency against the homogenizing forces of large-scale technology.

The common thread is unmistakable: control over data, over infrastructure, and over user experience is becoming the decisive competitive advantage. Those who set the rules—whether through copyright enforcement, open data policies, or the design of physical devices—will define the next phase of the AI era. The winners will be the ones who can navigate this landscape with clear strategies for ownership, openness, and human agency.
