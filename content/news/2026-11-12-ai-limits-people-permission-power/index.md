In a single day of news, three unrelated stories pointed at the same under-discussed fact about the AI buildout: its binding constraints are increasingly human, contractual, and physical rather than algorithmic. The models are still improving. What is slowing down is everything around them.

## A leadership vacuum at the center of the stack

Nvidia's ousted chief executive Jensen Huang has agreed to testify before Congress after senior lawmakers threatened a subpoena. The reported circumstances — an alleged affair with a woman suspected of spying for China — would make this a national-security question rather than a private corporate embarrassment, which is why the testimony matters beyond the company. President Trump's suggestion on Truth Social that Apple's Tim Cook should take the job is not a hiring plan; it is a signal of how much of the industry's sense of stability is being read through one person.

That is the real exposure. Nvidia's chips and roadmap anchor capital plans across cloud providers, model labs, and startups. When the top of that stack becomes unsettled, the uncertainty propagates into procurement schedules and multi-year contracts that assume continuity. Hugging Face's chief executive, meanwhile, has pledged to keep pressing for open-source AI — a fight that gets harder when the counterpart he was negotiating with is gone, and one that now hangs over questions about the reported $12.9 billion acquisition tied to Nvidia. Open-source advocates rarely win by argument alone; they win by having a sympathetic, empowered executive on the other side of the table.

## Delegation is easier than authorization

United Airlines said it will not allow customers to use AI agents to book flights or manage reservations, citing cybersecurity risk. The reasoning is more structural than it first appears. A booking is not an information request; it is a commitment of money and a change to a record governed by fare rules, refund policies, and consumer-protection obligations the airline is legally answerable for. A carrier can authenticate a human, apply fraud scoring, and manage chargebacks. A third-party agent acting on that human's behalf is harder to verify and harder to hold responsible when something goes wrong.

Contrast Phota Labs, which uses AI to automate photo editing for ordinary consumers. That product ships without asking anyone's permission, because it never crosses an organizational boundary. The pattern is consistent: AI features contained inside one product move quickly, while anything that acts across company lines needs agreed identity, liability allocation, and audit trails that mostly do not exist in consumer journeys yet.

Whoop's new social network points the same way from a different angle. Sharing sleep, recovery, and workout data is straightforward inside a first-party app; handing that data to a general-purpose agent requires permissions nobody has standardized.

## The power bill arrives

SoftBank reportedly lost more than $3 billion pre-ordering natural gas turbines to power AI data centers. Turbines are built by a small number of suppliers with multi-year lead times, so a pre-order is effectively a wager that demand will materialize on schedule. A loss that size is a warning to the next buyer: power is no longer a background assumption but a speculative position with real downside.

This shifts siting logic. If firm power is the scarce input, data centers may follow interconnection queues and pipeline access rather than latency to users — and financial losses, not forecasts, will discipline the next round of bets.

## What to watch

Nvidia's board and any named successor; the Congressional testimony calendar; whether other carriers follow United and whether payment networks or standards bodies issue a credential that makes delegated booking acceptable; whether turbine pre-orders convert into contracts; and whether open-source advocacy gains or loses leverage as the deal landscape shifts.

The technical frontier is still advancing. Deployment speed in 2026 will be set by named people, permission to act on someone's behalf, and firm power.
