The same week that Meta and a rival startup taught their AI assistants to phone a restaurant, a coalition including Google, Nvidia and Anthropic said it wants to find 100 gigawatts of grid capacity for new data centers. Those look like unrelated stories. They are the same story: artificial intelligence is moving from answering questions to taking actions, and every action has a physical or legal counterparty that has to be secured first.

## The demo is the cheap part

Instinct and Meta's Muse both now make outbound calls — booking tables, canceling subscriptions. The model side of that is largely solved; the friction sits with whoever is on the other end of the line. A reservation system expects a caller it can hold accountable, and a subscription desk may have terms requiring the account holder to cancel in person. An agent that sounds fluent still needs a legitimate claim to act on someone's behalf, plus a record proving it did what it claimed.

Multiply that across everything agents are being pointed at — payments, appointments, procurement, support tickets — and "agentic AI" becomes less a model problem than a permissions, identity and liability problem. That is slower, less demo-friendly engineering, and it is where the next round of differentiation is forming.

## Watts, wheels and phone lines

Emerald AI's stated target of 100 GW is the clearest signal of scale. That is on the order of a tenth of total U.S. generating capacity, and finding it means navigating interconnection queues, transmission limits and permitting timelines often measured in years rather than quarters. Chips do not run without power, and power does not appear because a model improved.

The same procurement logic runs through Lucid's tie-up with mobility platform Bolt for a European robotaxi service. The partnership is real; the vehicle orders are not yet placed. That gap between an announced alliance and a signed purchase order is precisely the kind of thing investors are learning to price differently.

Then there is Huawei, accelerating its Ascend 960DT AI chip toward a Q1 2027 launch as it tries to close China's AI computing gap with the U.S. and take on Nvidia. Export controls have turned compute into a geopolitical commodity, so the supply chain for training and inference is now a diplomatic question as much as an industrial one. Three very different headlines — a phone call, a power line, a fab roadmap — all describe the same hunt for scarce real-world inputs.

## Where the capital is going

Bain Capital Ventures' new $1.6 billion fund targets early-stage founders working on AGI and, notably, the infrastructure to run it efficiently — a bet that returns accrue to whoever makes scarce compute go further. Comp AI raised a $34 million Series A to push security and compliance toward continuous, agent-driven auditing, effectively automating the paperwork that lets businesses deploy AI at all. Spotify, meanwhile, expanded its podcast partner program to 35 new countries and says payouts have risen more than a third since it changed video creator terms in January — a reminder that distribution and monetization are being rebuilt alongside the models.

The pattern is consistent: money is flowing toward the layer that makes agents cheap, legal, powered and auditable, not merely clever.

## Why it matters

For a decade the binding constraint on AI was capability. Now it is throughput — interconnect queues, vehicle homologation, telephony rules, insurance underwriting, audit trails. These are institutional bottlenecks, and they do not respond to more GPUs. An advantage built on research talent can be eroded in a product cycle; an advantage built on grid interconnects, fleet operations and regulatory relationships is harder to copy. That favors incumbents with physical assets and creates openings for startups selling the permission layer to everyone else.

The caveat is that announcements are not capacity. A 100 GW aspiration, a robotaxi partnership without vehicle orders, and a chip with a 2027 launch date are all intentions. The industry's habit of pre-announcing the substrate it needs is itself a signal of how tight the real thing has become — and a reminder to read procurement commitments as claims to be verified, not delivered capacity.
