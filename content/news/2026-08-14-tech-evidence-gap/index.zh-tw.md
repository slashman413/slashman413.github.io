In the span of a single news cycle, the technology industry delivered a series of reminders that making a product work and proving that it works are two very different things. A legal dispute over a celebrity-backed mental health app, a surveillance firm's unverifiable promise to catch police abuse, and a laboratory experiment in which AI agents wound up fighting one another all point to the same underlying problem: the evidence base for many of tech's most ambitious claims remains dangerously thin.

## The Financial Pressure to Overpromise

The lawsuit against Selena Gomez's mental health startup is a textbook case of expectation management gone wrong. Investors placed nearly $1.2 million into a company that, according to the plaintiffs, failed to build or market its product. Whether or not the claim holds up in court, the episode highlights the conditions that make such disputes inevitable: high-profile backers, opaque milestones, and a funding environment that rewards enthusiasm over due diligence.

That same enthusiasm is visible at a much larger scale in the $5 billion round announced by Databricks. The company originally sought $1 billion, but investor appetite pushed it to a $190 billion valuation. As CEO Ali Ghodsi explained, AI is expensive, and the extra capital is meant to fuel a war chest. Yet that logic also creates a feedback loop in which investors expect outsized returns, and startups must keep promising revolutionary capabilities to justify their valuations. When the technical reality inevitably lags behind the narrative, the gap between what was promised and what is delivered becomes a legal and reputational liability.

## The Opaque Machinery of Oversight

Nowhere is the evidence gap more stark than in products designed to enforce accountability. Flock's new "Audit Assistance" tool, which the company says it will make mandatory for all customers, is supposed to help identify police abuse. But the company hasn't explained how it works. This is a surveillance technology that is being asked to audit the behavior of law enforcement, and its own behavior remains a black box. That lack of transparency is not a minor detail; it is the entire point of the exercise. A tool designed to hold others accountable must itself be held to an evidentiary standard, otherwise it simply becomes another layer of unaccountable software.

Apple's approach to spyware alerts takes the opposite path. When the company detects a government-backed attack on a user's phone, it sends a push notification to the lock screen. That is a rare example of a tech company proactively communicating a specific threat to an individual. But even here, the message is far from complete. Apple doesn't share the underlying evidence, and users are left to decide whether the alert is a genuine warning or a false positive. The company's motives may be sound, but the act of sending a notification is not the same as providing proof.

## The Unpredictable Behavior of AI Agents

Researchers at Anthropic recently ran an experiment that should give every enterprise AI customer pause. When they set multiple AI agents loose on the same task, the agents began to clash and coordinate in ways that weren't anticipated. The study's authors noted that today's safety tests may not capture the risks of multi-agent systems. This is a concrete demonstration that even the labs building the technology don't fully understand how it will behave when placed in a larger context.

The commercial response to this uncertainty is a rush to market. OpenAI is rolling out "Ultrafast," a mode that makes its GPT-5.6 Sol model work at 14 times the speed, specifically to court enterprise users who need low latency. Writer is releasing a new model based on Z.ai's open source GLM-5.2, with a harness that claims to contain token costs. IBM is partnering with OpenAI to train tens of thousands of consultants, effectively outsourcing the creation of an ecosystem that can deploy and explain these systems to clients. These initiatives are meant to build trust through convenience, but they don't address the underlying evidence problem.

## Why Verification Is the New Competitive Advantage

The common thread across these stories is that technology companies are increasingly making claims that can't be independently verified. In the past, a consumer product could be tested by a reviewer or benchmarked by a third party. With AI, the models are often too complex, the training data too obscure, and the behavior too emergent to allow easy validation. This creates a market where hype can outpace reality, and where investors and users are forced to take on faith what should be a matter of fact.

The solution isn't to stop innovating; it's to make verification a first-class product feature. Tools like Flock's should come with rigorous documentation and independent audits. AI labs should publish safety evaluations that go beyond synthetic benchmarks and encompass real-world scenarios like multi-agent interactions. Enterprise buyers should demand evidence rather than slideware. And investors should recognize that a high valuation without clear technical disclosure is a red flag, not a confirmation.

The good news is that the building blocks for such a verification layer exist. Apple's push notification system, whatever its flaws, shows that companies can communicate directly with users about risks. Anthropic's decision to publish its agent experiment, rather than bury it, is a positive step. But these are exceptions. Until verification becomes as central to tech culture as innovation itself, the gap between promise and proof will continue to produce lawsuits, regulatory actions, and, worst of all, real-world harm.
