In a single news cycle, Sequoia said it would fold two decades of offices and programs back into a smaller Bay Area firm while raising roughly $10 billion, Canva cut 22% of its staff, and Revolut's valuation rose 53% to $115 billion ahead of a US bank launch. Those look like three unrelated stories about venture capital, design software, and fintech. They are actually one story about where leverage sits in technology right now — and who gets to decide what a billion users see first.

## Retreat Is a Strategy, Not a Confession

Sequoia's move is easy to misread as retrenchment. But the fund size tells the opposite story: the firm is not abandoning ambition, it is abandoning dispersion. Closing regional outposts and internal programs while raising a $10 billion pool concentrates decision-making in fewer partners who hold larger positions in fewer companies. That is a different product than the one venture capital sold for twenty years — broad networks, many small checks, geographic optionality. The new pitch is depth: more capital per conviction, more operational involvement, less willingness to spread thin.

Canva's layoffs fit the same curve from the operating side. A company that grew on frictionless distribution and self-serve adoption now has to justify its cost base against slower growth, and 1,200-plus departures is what that arithmetic looks like at scale. Revolut's jump is the other half of the pattern: capital is concentrating on businesses that convert user reach into something durable and regulated, like a bank charter. The market is not paying for footprint. It is paying for defensibility.

## Defaults Are the New Chokepoint

The Google episode is the sharpest illustration. A reported plan to turn off Search for school systems that restrict AI drew criticism from New York mayor Zohran Mamdani and former FTC chair Lina Khan, who framed it as unregulated monopoly behavior. Set aside the merits of the policy for a moment: the notable fact is that a general-purpose utility can credibly be used as a bargaining chip in an unrelated negotiation. That is what control of a default looks like when it is exercised.

The same contest is playing out in living rooms. Titan OS raised $46 million to expand its smart TV operating system internationally, bringing total funding to $110 million — a bet that the home screen, not the content library, is the valuable surface. Whoever owns that layer controls discovery, advertising, and which apps are one click away and which are buried. Meanwhile Apple's Siri Recaps proved genuinely useful within a day of use, and also faintly unsettling: recaps are only possible because the assistant already sits across mail, messages, and calendar. The convenience and the dependency are the same feature, shipped together.

Why this matters more than any single announcement: the interface layer is where competition and consent get settled now. Product decisions taken there determine what millions of people can find without asking, and what they must opt out of rather than opt into.

## Where Growth Still Lives

The counterexample is instructive. India's microdrama market draws more than 118 million monthly viewers, and EloElo's Story TV — roughly 33 million active users — just took $80 million from NBCUniversal. That is real growth, but note its shape: a serialized format traveling faster than platforms do, with an incumbent media company buying in rather than building. Even the expansion story ends in concentration.

## What to Watch

Three tests will show whether narrowing produces better products or merely better margins. First, whether concentrated capital actually yields more durable companies, or just fewer, more fragile ones with less room to experiment. Second, how regulators treat conditional access — the practice of making a core service contingent on unrelated compliance — because that is the mechanism behind every defaults battle. Third, whether AI features that read your life come with controls designed before launch rather than after complaints. The direction of 2026 is clear enough: fewer bets, bigger ones, and more of the value captured at the point where users choose nothing at all.
