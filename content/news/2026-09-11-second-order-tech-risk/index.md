Two of today's headlines involve companies being hurt by decisions they did not make: Trezor's customers, exposed through an email provider it hired, and Nvidia's growth forecast, which depends on customers whose own economics are unsettled. The pattern is not new, but it is now the dominant way value and risk get allocated in technology. What matters increasingly is not the product you build, but the layer you sit on and the layers sitting on you.

## The vendor you never chose

Trezor confirmed a data breach at an email provider it relies on, the second such incident involving a supplier, and scammers are now targeting hundreds of thousands of crypto owners. The detail worth sitting with is where the failure occurred. A hardware wallet's entire pitch is that private keys never touch a networked machine. That engineering claim held. The exposure came instead from a marketing list, a CRM, an outbound email tool, one step to the side of the cryptographic core.

Security boundaries, in other words, are drawn by procurement contracts and vendor risk reviews, not by architecture diagrams. Every company that outsources a function inherits the weakest security posture in its supply chain, and it often cannot audit that posture directly. This is why breach disclosures increasingly read as supply-chain news rather than product news.

## Growth that depends on someone else's budget

Jensen Huang says Nvidia will grow roughly 70% next year and insists its deals are not circular. The insistence is itself informative. Nvidia invests in AI labs and cloud providers that then buy Nvidia hardware, so the question is not whether the money literally loops back but how reflexive the demand is. If a meaningful share of GPU purchases is funded by capital that originated with the seller, the growth is real in accounting terms and fragile in economic ones.

OpenAI's decision to pause Pro subscription sign-ups because of capacity strain illustrates the other edge of the same dynamic. Demand is outstripping supply badly enough that the company is turning away revenue rather than degrading service, which is a genuine constraint rather than a marketing decision, and it implies Nvidia's order book has room. But it also means OpenAI's growth rate is set by chip and datacenter availability, not by its ability to sell.

Anthropic's report alleging sustained distillation campaigns by Alibaba, Moonshot AI, and DeepSeek points at a subtler dependency. When a model's outputs can be harvested and used to train a competitor, capability becomes partly transferable. That weakens the link between spending on compute and durable advantage, and it explains why labs increasingly treat inference as something to meter, watermark, and monitor rather than simply sell.

Three stories, one shape: the entity reporting strength is rarely the entity controlling the input.

## Geography, arenas, and rented distribution

Furo's three founders left Silicon Valley for Germany and still raised $4 million, mostly from American backers. That is a small round, but it demonstrates that capital is now more mobile than talent, and that a lower local cost base can be a structural advantage rather than a concession. Where a company is built matters less than it did; what it can afford per engineer matters more.

Collaborative Fund's purchase of a stake in D.C. United and its stadium, following Thrive Capital's lead into sports ownership, is a different kind of layer play. The pitch is that a stadium and a fanbase become a showcase and distribution channel for portfolio companies. Mark Wahlberg's appearance at Disrupt, framed around discussing attendees' work rather than his own, is the celebrity-investor version of the same idea: attention as infrastructure.

Meta's Muse becoming the number two app in the United States while still trailing the early trajectories of Threads and Meta AI is a useful counterweight. Distributive muscle is not fungible across product categories, and a captive audience does not automatically convert into habitual use.

## What to watch

The practical question for anyone evaluating a tech company today is not only what it builds, but which inputs it rents, which vendors it trusts, and how much of its reported growth arrives through a partner's books. Second-order risk is inexpensive to ignore and costly to discover. That asymmetry, more than any single product cycle, is what the week's headlines actually describe.
